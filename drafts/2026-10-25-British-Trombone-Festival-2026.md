## Special Update

Welcome to the first Special Update! As you may have guessed from the title and irregular posting time of this post, I’ll be making these updates whenever something out of the ordinary happens. In this case, it’s because I headed to the Royal Birmingham Conservatoire (RBC) on **October 24th and 25th** for the bi-annual British Trombone Festival, hosted by the British Trombone Society (BTS).

Ironically enough, I almost couldn’t make it to the festival because my train to Birmingham was cancelled on the morning of my departure on the **24th**. The one thing that saved me from having to spend money on another train or bus ticket last-minute was a new British government policy enacted just last month, which stated that ticketholders can ride any alternative train to their destination in the two hours before or after their original train if it was cancelled. Thanks to this, I was able to make it to Birmingham on time, albeit with an added transfer (my original train was direct).

After I got to Birmingham and registered my attendance for the festival, my first order of business for the festival was doing some quick warmups with the other attendees, before sitting in on a theatre playing masterclass with Christopher Augustine, the trombonist for the West End’s production of Wicked (which I incidentally watched a couple of years ago). I then listened to a jazz performance by the British Army’s Household Division Trombone Ensemble, and had a short lunch break in time for a breathing seminar hosted by a certain RNCM trombone tutor, which I found surprisingly useful in visualising how I breathe when playing.

Unfortunately, I wasn’t able to attend a couple of events due to some personal business I needed to deal with urgently, but I managed to make it in time for the massed trombone choir rehearsal for the second day's gala performance. That was soon followed by a panel discussion with some of the festival’s presenters and guests about all aspects of trombone playing, and the first day concluded with a double jazz act of Elliot Mason and Rory Ingram’s Trombone Assembly, two show-stopping displays of improvisational mastery that may very well have made me want to pick up jazz again after a long hiatus.

***

The **25th** started with my first-ever Greggs sausage roll for breakfast. Much to my delight, it lived up to the hype as some much-needed comfort food before I tried out some Rath trombones and headed into the warm-up session. I then listened to a lecture by former ITV and Classical FM presenter John Suchet about Beethoven and the trombone, with a particular focus on the hilarious context behind the composition of his Three Equali (to keep things brief, it involved a spa trip gone wrong and forbidden love). This ended with a performance of said Equali by Suchet and the Concert Trombone Quartette, who'll make a return later.

More historical music-making soon followed when I next went to a presentation hosted by another RNCM trombone/sackbut tutor about 19th and early 20th-century trombones, where I discovered that I had played the repertoire being used to demonstrate these instruments during our studio class just over a month ago. Still, hearing it being played on historical trombones (and guest tuba) was quite a unique experience.

After another mass trombone choir rehearsal and lunch, I attended a recital by Josh Cirtina, who played some women-composed pieces from his upcoming album along with three other trombonists studying at the Royal College of Music. While most of it was contemporary music (which I did enjoy), there was a piece by Barbara Strozzi in the mix, continuing the second day's 'historical music' theme. This culminated in the Concert Trombone Quartette's lecture recital on historical trombone chamber music, which included an audience participation session where we could play on the actual historical instruments these pieces were written for. Even though I wasn't able to play Beethoven's equali due to there being no bass sackbuts/trombones to lend out, I still got to play a German Romantic-era trombone for a Bruckner piece. Compared to a modern trombone, the German Romantic trombone had a trigger that was controlled by a thumb loop attached to a drawstring, and required significantly more air to play. This was most prevalent in the higher register, which left my chops rather busted by the end of the Brucker, though I managed to play most of it without issue.

The festival ended with a few performances from the musicians of the RBC, before coming to a close with the mass trombone choir, and it's here that I'll reveal what we all played for that: trombone choir arrangements of the theme song from the British children's TV show The Wombles, and Dynamite by K-Pop group BTS (seeing as the British Trombone Society was the original BTS, after all). Since Elliot Mason joined us on-stage for the latter piece, I can safely say I've shared the stage with two jazz trombone legends now (with Al Kay being the first back at ITF 2025).

On that note, it's certainly obvious why the British Trombone Festival isn't as large-scale as its international counterpart, but I still had a lot of fun and learned more than a few useful things about the trombone there, so it was definitely a positive and unforgettable experience for me. My gracious and heartfelt thanks to the British Trombone Society (aka the original BTS) for putting this festival together, and I can't wait to see what they'll be organising in lieu of a festival next year!
