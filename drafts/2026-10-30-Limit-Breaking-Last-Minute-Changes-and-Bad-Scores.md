# Limit Breaking, Last-Minute Changes, and Bad Scores

**TL;DR:**
- A new approach to practicing pushed me to my limits
- I taught myself why last-minute changes are a bad idea
- Faulty scores messed up sackbut rehearsal

## Life Update

(Since I didn't take many photos this week, here's some more from my trip to Birmingham)

### Music Things

Even though I mainly spent it playing excerpts and pieces I had already done before, I was still pushed to my limits during my lesson this week. This was primarily due to being challenged to take less breaths when playing, which quickly left me gasping for air, although it also allowed me to convey my musical ideas more smoothly. Since I don't leave myself in such a state that often, which would certainly help me develop my stamina and air capacity, this experience made me wonder if I could incorporate something along these lines into my practice sessions to further test and improve myself.

This week's studio class was a classic 'play-to-the-class' session with an RNCM twist: we were provided with a college accompanist. While I had a couple other potential pieces in mind for this class, I ultimately decided to play the first movement of Gordon Jacob's trombone concerto, since I believed its mixture of lyrical and technical sections would provide me with the greatest amount of feedback. This being a last-minute change meant I had less time to brush up on the piece than intended, so my resulting performance was noticeably worse than either my recital or my Guildhall audition video, but I did receive some useful pointers on my playing style in the end. Ironically enough, this year's Guildhall auditions was why  one of my fellow trombones played this piece for the session as well, and certainly sounded better than my rusty rendition.

Having obtained a small shank trombone mouthpiece a few weeks back, my sackbut lessons could now proceed as planned, though I have yet to fully adapt to my new bass sackbut being pitched in F rather than Eb like my previous one. This naturally resulted in my existing muscle memory working against me whenever I tried to sight read, which was an issue that persisted into sackbut ensemble rehearsal later in the day. However, that was only the second-most pressing issue during rehearsal, as we discovered that the score and the parts we were reading had different metres and barlines, despite being published by the same company! While we still managed to play something coherent by the end, I felt that trying to work around this engraving error made this particular rehearsal less productive than it should have been.

## Practice Room

In this week's practice, I decided to recreate how I felt during this week's lesson by trying to breathe only when absolutely necessary, rather than the moment I needed more air. While this approach is immensely exhausting, it forces me to 'do more with less' by maximising the amount and duration of notes I play with each breath, thereby making my playing more efficient over time. One interesting side effect of this approach is that I can feel which parts of my body tire out first whenever I restrict my oxygen intake, allowing me to devise workarounds for minimising the effort I exert in turn.

After only a couple of days of trying it out, this approach has already yielded some results for me, as I found that I needed less effort than before to make a good sound during ensemble playing. Still, I know I can go much further using this practice method, so I'll be complementing it with everything else I've learned up to this point.
