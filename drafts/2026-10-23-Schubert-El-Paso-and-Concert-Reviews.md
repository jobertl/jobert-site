## Life Update

### Music Things

I participated in my first project at the RNCM, which was an informal performance of Schubert's Unfinished Symphony with a Repertoire Orchestra (for UBC readers, think of it like Orchestral Readings with a performance aspect to it). Since Repertoire Orchestra projects are intended to give RNCM conducting students podium time while familiarising the rest of us with orchestral repertoire (hence their name), their entire 'cycle' (for lack of a better word, I'm borrowing UBC terminology again) from rehearsals to performance lasts around a week, so they are shorter than other projects.

Even so, this lower-stakes project didn't mean us students were exempt from the RNCM's usual high standards, as we were helpfully reminded by a faculty member to prepare more thoroughly due to a lacklustre start in our first rehearsal, which included listening through the piece with a score and our parts. Since being ready to play through a piece flawlessly by the first rehearsal is the norm for musicians in British orchestras, the RNCM faculty expect us to rise to this level as well, especially as they already consider us to be professionals. While fully adjusting to this standard of excellence will take a bit more time, I still managed to meet expectations along with the rest of the orchestra by the end of this rehearsal, but I'll definitely put in a lot more effort for the remainder of this project and future ones.

The second and third rehearsals took place on the same day as the performance itself, which meant it was a rather busy day for me. Thankfully, the trombones don't do much playing in the Unfinished Symphony, though we do have some nice parts, so my chops weren't destroyed by all this 'playing'. With the performance itself being rather smooth, I'm happy to report I survived my first official RNCM concert (informal as it may be).

Aside from devising exercises for excerpts in this week's studio class, the RNCM trombones also welcomed several guests from the US' University of Texas El Paso (UTEP), seeing as they were touring around the UK while being based in Leeds for a series of performances. After our regular studio time ended, almost all of us stayed behind to read through some trombone choir pieces with the UTEP trombones (including an arrangement of the 'march' movement from Tchaikovsky 6!), before having some beers at the RNCM bar. Even though I was based in North America up to a couple of months ago, I didn't work with many Texan trombonists during my time there, so this was a pretty good opportunity to get a glimpse into their unique musical culture. Of course, it goes without saying that they were *very* American as Texans, but the enthusiasm they brought to the RNCM was more than enough to smooth over any initial culture shock.

## Concert Review

While I've teased about making it an actual thing before, I'm happy to announce that I've finally added a concert review section to my blog posts! However, unlike my existing baked pork chop rice review series, no reviews here will be assigned a rating, since reviewing more types of ensemble performance than food items means any possible criteria will be all over the place.

Having obtained free tickets from an instructor of ours who plays there, some of the RNCM trombones went to this week's Hallé concert (incidentally, we also met fellow RNCM students who didn't had an instructor to give them free tickets, so they paid the student price). Alongside the world premiere of an orchestration of Ravel's Three Songs Without Words and Szymanowski's Second Violin Concerto performed by Hallé orchestra leader Emily Davis, the concert's main draw was Rachmaninoff's Third Symphony, all under the baton of guest conductor Nicholas Collon.

Despite Ravel not being the orchestrator for it (funnily enough, an orchestration for the third song was discovered in a French library a few months ago and will be published in 2027), I thought that this orchestration of Three Songs Without Words was up to his standards, since it expertly leveraged the strengths of each instrument to paint a vivid picture of what each song was conveying. How the third song ended was a highlight of this orchestration, as the conductor cued all instruments to stop playing, while letting the tubular bells continue to resound throughout the concert hall, concluding the piece with a Christmas-y feel that perfectly encapsulates its theme of opening presents.

While I personally wasn't a fan of the Szymanowski violin concerto (in fact, I much preferred the Bartok duet played as an encore), mainly due to it being a one-movement work and thus sounding rather formless, I couldn't help but genuinely admire Davis' interpretation of the piece and the passion she put into it. In particular, her technical execution was flawless throughout, and the sections where she could take advantage of them to bring out the violin's true expressive qualities were my favourite parts of the piece.

With Rachmaninoff's Third Symphony being a rather unorthodox one, primarily due to having only three movements instead of the usual four, it definitely felt a bit off to my ears when compared to my top picks. Still, this didn't stop me from appreciating the hallmarks of the Russian compositional style sprinkled in throughout, particularly how ornate it sounded at times, and the final bars of the last movement could definitely give Tchaikovsky's Fourth a run for its money.

(Side note: Since it looked rather concise and efficient, letting him direct the orchestra with as little movement as possible, I really enjoyed watching Mastro Collon conduct. Hopefully, I'll have more chances to see him do so in the future.)
